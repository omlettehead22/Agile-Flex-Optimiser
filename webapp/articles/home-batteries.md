A home battery stores electricity when it's cheap or free and releases it when it's expensive. Whether that pays for itself comes down to one question: how big is the gap between the price you store at and the price you avoid?

## Three ways a battery saves money

1. **Storing spare solar.** Without a battery, unused daytime solar is exported for a modest payment. With one, it powers your evening instead, saving the full import price.
2. **Charging from the grid at night.** On a time-of-use tariff like Agile, a battery can fill up overnight at low prices and run the house through the 4pm to 7pm peak.
3. **Using negative prices.** When prices go below zero, the battery fills up and you're paid for it.

The best results usually come from combining them: solar in summer, cheap overnight charging in winter when solar is weak.

## What eats into the savings

- **Losses:** roughly 10% of the energy is lost in a charge-and-discharge cycle.
- **Cost:** batteries often cost several thousand pounds installed. Get quotes.
- **Lifetime:** many come with a 10-year warranty. A battery that takes 12 years to pay back is a poor deal.
- **Size:** a battery much bigger than your evening use sits half-empty. Match it to what you use between about 4pm and bedtime.

## A simple test

Take the daily gap between peak and off-peak prices you'd actually see, multiply by the energy you'd shift each day, then by 365. Compare that yearly saving with the installed cost. For example, shifting 5 kWh across a 20p gap saves about £1 a day, or about £365 a year.

The [solar calculator](/#solar) shows how a battery changes the result when you have panels. Try it with and without the battery: sometimes panels alone pay back faster.

## Batteries without solar

They can work on Agile and similar tariffs, because of the overnight-to-peak gap. But that gap varies with the wholesale market, so savings are less predictable than with solar. Be cautious of sales pitches that assume today's best days happen every day.
